\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

Music, podcasts and audiobooks can form part of the same person's listening
activities, while being accessed through different applications. In such a
situation, finding audio involves choosing both what to listen to and where
to look for it. A listener may save an item in one application, continue a
different item elsewhere and later need to remember which application holds
each one. These possible organisational difficulties motivate the project.

Finding content is only one part of this problem. Saving an interesting item
should also make it possible to find that item again. Similarly, returning to
an unfinished podcast or audiobook requires a recognisable route back to the
content and its listening position. For example, a student who stops listening
to an audiobook during a study break should be able to identify the unfinished
book and continue it later.

Combining the three audio types could provide a common place for these
activities, but it also raises a design question. A mixed collection may be
difficult to navigate if its content types are unclear or if too many items
compete for attention. Music tracks, podcast episodes and audiobook chapters
also need to remain identifiable within a shared interface. Common navigation
and saving controls therefore have to be balanced with clear categories and
information about each item.

Existing services already take different approaches to this organisation. Spotify combines music, podcasts and audiobooks in one service, while keeping the content types identifiable within its listening experience \cite{spotifyaudiobooks2025}. Apple offers separate applications for music, podcasts and audiobooks through Apple Music, Apple Podcasts and Apple Books \cite{appleapps2026}. These examples show that combining audio content is not a new idea, and neither approach is automatically easier for every user. Omnisound instead explores a specific interface question: whether shared search, a common library and clear content categories can make it easier to find saved material and continue unfinished listening without confusing the different audio types.

Omnisound is the project's response to this question: a web-interface
prototype for finding, saving and resuming audio in one organised place. It
primarily targets students who regularly listen to music, podcasts or
audiobooks, including listeners who use several content types and those who
mainly listen to music. The project investigates how the interface can help
these listeners keep track of saved and unfinished content.

The initial high-fidelity prototype (V1) is designed primarily for a desktop
browser. It
provides a shared search, content-type filters, a library of saved items and a
Continue Listening area. Its two main task flows are finding and saving
an item, then locating it in the library, and resuming an unfinished podcast
or audiobook. These flows give the project a specific interaction focus
within the broader audio concept.

\section{Purpose}
\label{sec:purpose}

The purpose of the project is to investigate how a unified audio interface
can support finding, organising and resuming different types of audio content
while keeping the experience clear and manageable for the intended users.
The motivation is to explore whether a common search and library can reduce
the effort of returning to wanted content without making the differences
between music, podcasts and audiobooks harder to understand.

This investigation also provides an opportunity to apply user-centred design
within the HCI course. The course project requires an iterative process and
usability testing in at least one iteration \cite[pp.~2--3]{p3project}.
For Omnisound, the purpose is therefore pursued through requirements,
alternative designs, prototypes and evaluation. The desired qualities are
ease of learning, clear organisation and useful feedback; whether the design
supports these qualities must be assessed through the project work.

\section{Goals}
\label{sec:goals}

The first goal was to identify what students need from an application that brings music, podcasts and audiobooks together. We wanted to focus on the functions that matter most, such as finding audio, saving content for later and continuing something that was not finished. These needs would help us decide what to include in Omnisound and give us a way to evaluate the interface beyond how it looks.

Another goal was to explore different design ideas before developing the interactive prototypes. We considered different ways to organise audio categories and display content, such as separate categories or a mixed view with filters, and cards or lists. The early sketches helped us explore these ideas, while V1 and V2 showed how the design developed into an interface that users could interact with.

The final goal was to test how well people could use Omnisound. We wanted to see whether users could complete the main tasks, where they experienced difficulties and which parts of the interface needed improvement. The first usability test helped us decide what to change in V2, and the second test allowed us to compare the two versions. Together, the sketches, prototypes and usability tests helped us explain our design choices and what we learned from the project.

\section{Delimitations}
\label{sec:delimitations}

Omnisound is an interface prototype. The project focuses on navigation,
content organisation and the visibility of actions and listening progress.
It does not implement music-streaming infrastructure, integration with
commercial audio services, payment or subscription processing, licensing
systems, backend databases or production account management. The prototype
is implemented only as a front end. Limiting the implementation in this way
allows the design work to concentrate on the
selected interactions within the course project.

Search uses a sample catalogue, and the player controls and displayed progress
represent the intended listening experience. They are sufficient to explore
how users move through the interface, recognise content and interpret
feedback. They do not demonstrate reliable audio delivery, synchronisation
between devices or the operation of a real streaming service. Conclusions
must therefore concern interaction with the prototype rather than the
technical performance of a complete product.

The task scope is also limited. Search and save, retrieval from the library
and continuation of unfinished podcasts or audiobooks receive priority.
Other possible activities, such as managing a production account or exploring
a large commercial catalogue, are outside this evaluation focus. The primary
context is desktop use, so the project cannot establish usability across all
mobile devices or listening environments. Likewise, conclusions drawn from
evaluation with the intended student group should be considered in relation
to that group and the tasks tested.

Following the first usability test, a second version of Omnisound was
developed to address the problems identified during evaluation. The changes
focused on reducing the size of the Home banner, making Continue Listening
easier to discover, and
improving the clarity of the Save/Saved control. A second usability test was
then conducted using V2 and the same three core tasks, allowing the group to
compare the two iterations. The testing procedures and findings are
presented in the Method and Results chapters.
Since Omnisound remains an interface prototype, the conclusions concern the
tested interactions rather than the technical performance of a complete
streaming service.

\section{Division of Work}
\label{sec:division-of-work}

The project was carried out by Obid Alrhman Aljabi, Yacoub Dawli and Sarshar Chavoshi Rad.

Yacoub Dawli was responsible for leading the interviews and moderating the usability tests. Obid Alrhman Aljabi and Sarshar Chavoshi Rad were responsible for documenting participant responses and observations. During usability testing, Obid focused on participant behaviour and comments, while Sarshar documented task completion times and navigation steps.

The group collaborated on analysing user needs, discussing requirements and evaluating different interface designs. The design process included low-fidelity sketches, two high-fidelity prototypes and usability evaluations. Findings from the first evaluation informed the changes introduced in the second prototype.

The project findings and design decisions were discussed within the group and formed the basis for the report.

\section{User-Centred Design and Iteration}
\label{sec:ucd-iteration}

User-centred design begins with understanding the people who will use an
interface, what they want to achieve and the circumstances in which they
will use it. Rogers, Sharp and Preece explain that understanding people's
activities and differences helps designers question assumptions about their
needs \cite[pp.~16--17]{interactiondesign2023}. A target group therefore
provides a starting point for research, rather than a reason to assume that
all its members have the same habits or expectations.

User needs and goals guide the requirements for an interface. Designers can
then explore alternative ways to support those goals and represent the ideas
in prototypes. Evaluation with intended users examines whether a proposed
solution works as expected. A prototype is a candidate solution that can be
tested by observing people using it \cite[Sec.~``What Is a Testable
Prototype?'']{perniceprototypes}. This makes evaluation part of design,
rather than a check reserved for the finished product.

Iteration connects evaluation to further design decisions. Findings can lead
to revised requirements, a different alternative or changes to an existing
interface, followed by another evaluation \cite[Sec.~``Why Test a
Prototype?'']{perniceprototypes}. Simply producing several versions does not
establish this connection. A meaningful iteration should explain which
problem motivated a change and examine whether the problem remains. This
principle is relevant to Omnisound's student audience and its two evaluated
high-fidelity prototype iterations.

\section{Usability and User Experience}
\label{sec:usability-ux}

ISO 9241-11:2018 relates usability to specified users, goals and contexts of
use through effectiveness, efficiency and satisfaction. These definitions
are reproduced in ISO/TR 25060:2023 \cite[Sec.~3.1]{isocif2023}. Usability
concerns what people can achieve with a system in a particular situation;
it cannot be established from appearance alone.

Effectiveness concerns accurate and complete achievement of a goal, while
efficiency concerns the resources used in relation to that outcome
\cite[Secs.~3.1.7--3.1.8]{isocif2023}. In an audio interface,
effectiveness could involve successfully saving an item and finding it again.
Efficiency concerns the time and effort required to reach that outcome.
Successful completion and a straightforward route are thus different aspects
of the same task. Satisfaction concerns how well the experience of use meets
the user's needs and expectations \cite[Sec.~3.1.9]{isocif2023}.

Learnability and utility are additional useful HCI concepts. Learnability
concerns how easily a person can learn to use an interface, including getting
started with unfamiliar controls. Utility concerns whether the available
functions support what users need to do \cite[p.~21]{interactiondesign2023}.
For example, a clearly labelled search may be easy to learn, while a way to
return to unfinished audio addresses a functional need. These concepts
complement the three ISO dimensions; they are not additional components of
the ISO definition of usability.

User experience includes people's perceptions and responses before, during
and after interaction, including anticipated use
\cite[Sec.~3.1.15]{isocif2023}. Satisfaction and frustration are relevant
examples. Task completion alone cannot explain how an interaction feels.
Together, these concepts provide a basis for examining searching, saving,
retrieving and resuming audio without treating one measure as a complete
assessment of the interface.

\section{Interface Design Principles}
\label{sec:interface-principles}

Visibility means making the options needed for a task apparent to the user
\cite[p.~28]{interactiondesign2023}. An available function has limited value
if people cannot discover it. In an audio interface, recognisable navigation
and information about listening progress can help users identify where to
go and what can be continued.

Feedback communicates the outcome of an action and the current system state
\cite[Sec.~1]{nielsenheuristics}. After saving an item, a visible confirmation
can help the user understand that the action succeeded. Feedback should be
timely and clear enough to support the next decision, such as continuing to
browse or opening the saved collection.

Consistency means using similar terms, controls and behaviour for similar
interactions, both within a product and in relation to familiar conventions
\cite[Sec.~4]{nielsenheuristics}. A shared saving action across different audio
types can support predictable interaction. Consistency still allows relevant
differences in content, such as episode titles and audiobook chapters.

Affordances concern the actions possible in the relationship between a
person and an object. Signifiers are perceivable cues that communicate what
can be done \cite{normansignifiers}. In a graphical interface, a button label,
icon or visual treatment can signify an available action. A control's
appearance and the action it actually supports should agree. This distinction
helps explain why making an action available and making it understandable
are separate design concerns.

\section{Cognition and Mental Models}
\label{sec:cognition-mental-models}

A mental model is a user's understanding of how a system works. It supports
predictions about what an action will do and develops through previous
experience \cite[Sec.~``What Are Mental Models?'']{chanmentalmodels}. Users
and designers may have different models of the same interface. Familiarity
with another audio service may, for example, shape expectations about where
saved items belong or whether opening unfinished content will resume it.
Clear category names and navigation can help communicate how a shared audio
collection is organised.

Attention concerns which information people notice while pursuing a goal.
Layout and visual prominence matter because competing information can reduce
the visibility of relevant content \cite[Sec.~8]{nielsenheuristics}. A large
introductory area and a route back to unfinished listening may compete for
attention on the same screen. This provides a reason to examine the relative
prominence of elements, without assuming that size alone determines what
every user notices.

Recognition over recall means displaying options and information that users
can identify, rather than requiring them to remember details
\cite[Sec.~6]{nielsenheuristics}. Visible content categories, item titles and
progress information can support this recognition. For an audio collection,
these cues may help someone distinguish saved material from unfinished
listening and choose a suitable next action.

\section{User Research and Evaluation Methods}
\label{sec:research-evaluation}

Qualitative interviews explore people's experiences, needs and expectations.
Open questions and follow-up questions allow participants to explain what
matters to them in their own terms \cite{rosalainterviews}. Interviews can
inform requirements, but they collect accounts of behaviour rather than
direct observations of interface use. Combining these accounts with
observations can provide a fuller basis for understanding users.

Affinity diagramming organises related observations or ideas into groups
that express common themes \cite[Sec.~``Defining Affinity
Diagramming'']{krauseaffinity}. It can help a design team make sense of
interview notes and identify connections between needs. The grouping is an
interpretation of the material, so observations that differ from a common
theme should also remain visible in the analysis.

Personas are research-informed representations of users' goals, needs and
expectations. Qualitative personas draw on patterns in research to help a
team focus its design decisions \cite{laubheimerpersonas}. They summarise
relevant differences between users rather than reproducing every interview
as a separate profile. A persona based only on assumptions should not be
treated as evidence about the intended audience, and a qualitative persona
does not establish how common a user type is in the wider population.

Low-fidelity prototypes, such as paper sketches, represent early ideas with
limited detail. They allow designers to explore layouts and navigation before
investing in a detailed interface, and can be changed or discarded readily
\cite[Sec.~``Benefits of Low-Fidelity Prototypes'']{perniceprototypes}.
More detailed, interactive prototypes allow evaluation of specific controls
and task flows \cite[Sec.~``Benefits of High-Fidelity
Prototypes'']{perniceprototypes}. The appropriate level of detail depends on
the question being investigated; visual polish alone does not establish that
a design meets users' needs.

Think-aloud usability testing asks participants to express their thoughts
while completing representative tasks with an interface
\cite[Sec.~``Defining Thinking Aloud Testing'']{nielsenthinkaloud}.
Observations of actions, hesitation and comments can reveal difficulties and
help explain why a participant chose a particular route. These observations
can inform redesign, including changes to the visibility or interpretation
of controls.

Qualitative usability tests also have limitations. Speaking aloud creates
an unusual situation, and prompts can influence behaviour
\cite[Sec.~``Downsides of Think-Aloud'']{nielsenthinkaloud}.
Findings from a small group can identify problems in the tested interactions,
but do not automatically provide statistically generalisable conclusions.
Task times and navigation steps should therefore be interpreted alongside
observations and the test context, rather than treated as universal measures
of performance.

\section{Research Ethics}
\label{sec:research-ethics}

User research should respect participants' ability to make an informed and
voluntary decision. Vetenskapsr\aa{}det's \emph{God forskningssed 2024}
explains that people should understand what participation involves, how
information will be handled and how to withdraw consent
\cite[pp.~62--63]{vetenskapsradet2024}. Information should be understandable
to the intended participants, who should have an opportunity to ask questions
and decline or stop participation without pressure.

Privacy requires responsible handling of participant information. Only
information needed for the study should be collected, and it should be
protected appropriately. Removing names alone may not prevent identification
when other details can be combined \cite[pp.~66--67]{vetenskapsradet2024}.
This matters when selecting background details, quotations and observations
for a report.

Accurate reporting is also an ethical responsibility. Observations and
measurements should be presented honestly, with interpretations and
limitations made clear \cite[pp.~11--12]{vetenskapsradet2024}.
Results that challenge a design should be retained rather than adjusted to
support a preferred conclusion. These principles provide the basis for
describing the group's documented ethical procedures in the Method chapter.

\section{Overall Design Process}
\label{sec:overall-design-process}

The project combined early concept development, design alternatives,
prototyping and two rounds of usability testing. The concept and paper
sketches established the intended listening activities and possible ways to
organise the interface. These ideas were developed into the first
high-fidelity prototype, V1. Its evaluation provided a basis for a targeted
redesign, which produced V2. A second usability test then examined the updated
interface using the same core task goals. Figure~\ref{fig:method-process}
summarises this sequence.

This approach was suitable because combining audio types raises questions
about how people understand categories, saved content and listening progress.
An interactive prototype allowed the group to observe those decisions during
use. The user-centred approach described in Section~\ref{sec:ucd-iteration}
therefore informed evaluation of concrete tasks. Iteration connected the
observed problems to changes and another evaluation; creating a second
version alone would not have provided that connection.

\section{Initial Design Studio}
\label{sec:initial-design-studio}

The early design work was documented through concept material and
low-fidelity sketches. The Session 4 concept sheet set out a shared audio
interface for students, with finding, saving and returning to content as its
main activities. It also recorded alternative approaches to organisation:
separate category tabs compared with a mixed feed and filters, and list
views compared with cards. These alternatives framed the question of how to
combine content while keeping its different types recognisable.

The original paper sketches explored a navigation area, grouped audio
categories and a player layout. Together, they made it possible to examine
where navigation, content and playback controls could fit before committing
to a detailed interface. They provided concrete material for discussing
interface ideas, without requiring each alternative to be fully implemented.

At this stage, the focus was the organisation of activities and information.
The concept material connected possible layouts to shared search, a common
library and continued listening. Keeping the drawings simple supported
consideration of these relationships before visual detail and interactive
behaviour became the main focus. The sketches and selected design are
presented in the Design chapter.

\section{Data Collection}
\label{sec:method-data-collection}

To better understand what students might want from a combined audio application, the group prepared an interview plan during Project Session 2. The questions focused on which audio applications people normally used, what they liked about them, what could be improved and what they would expect from an application that brings music, podcasts and audiobooks together. The plan also included showing early interface ideas and asking about first impressions, missing features and how users would expect to complete common tasks.

Yacoub Dawli was assigned to lead the interviews, while Obid Alrhman Aljabi and Sarshar Chavoshi Rad were assigned to take handwritten notes. The questions were mostly open-ended so that participants could explain their habits and preferences in their own words. This approach was useful because the group wanted to understand what people expected from an audio interface before deciding how it should be organised \cite{rosalainterviews}.

During the V1 and V2 usability tests, we also asked participants about their listening habits. We wanted to know which applications they normally used, whether they listened mainly to music, podcasts or audiobooks, and which devices they preferred. Their answers gave us useful background information about the participants. Together with their comments and our observations during the tasks, this helped us understand how they experienced Omnisound.

\section{Data Analysis and Requirements}
\label{sec:method-analysis-requirements}

The initial Omnisound concept and the chosen prototype tasks helped the group decide which functions were most important. The interface needed to let users find audio content, save items for later, return to their saved collection and continue listening to something they had not finished. It was also important that users could easily tell the difference between music, podcasts and audiobooks, even though all three were available in the same application.

Rather than trying to build every feature found in a commercial audio service, the group focused on a few tasks that users could complete from beginning to end. For example, finding an artist was connected to saving that content and checking that it appeared in the Library. Resuming an audiobook required users to find unfinished content and return to the previous listening position. Browsing podcasts was another simple way to examine whether the different audio categories were easy to find. These tasks helped determine which screens, controls and interactions the prototypes needed.

The group also had to decide what could reasonably be included within the scope of a university project. Features such as subscriptions, account management and real audio streaming were left outside the prototype because the main focus was the interface and how people interacted with it. The later usability tests helped identify which parts of the interface were clear and which needed improvement. Although personas and affinity diagrams are discussed as HCI methods in Chapter 2, no completed outputs from those methods are documented in the project material, so they are not presented as steps used in the requirements analysis.

\subsection{User Goals}

The user goals describe what people should be able to do with Omnisound. Based on the project concept and the main activities selected for the prototype, we focused on four goals: finding audio content, saving something for later, returning to unfinished listening and browsing different audio categories without confusion.

For example, someone should be able to find an artist, save it and then locate it again in Library. Similarly, someone who has not finished an audiobook should be able to find it and continue from the previous position. These goals helped us decide which functions and screens to include in the prototype.

\subsection{Usability Goals}

Alongside the user goals, we wanted the main interactions to be easy to understand and complete. We focused on effectiveness, efficiency and learnability. This meant examining whether users could complete the tasks, how much time and navigation they needed, and whether they understood the interface without being shown which buttons to use.

We also wanted actions to provide clear feedback. For example, when someone saves an item, the interface should make it clear that the item has been saved and where it can be found again. During usability testing, we examined these goals through task completion, completion times, navigation routes, hesitation, participant comments and the need for assistance.

\section{Prototyping Methods}
\label{sec:method-prototyping}

Low-fidelity sketches and high-fidelity prototypes served different purposes.
The paper drawings explored layouts and relationships between navigation,
audio categories and playback. They allowed interface ideas to be considered
before the group invested in detailed screens. This use of simple drawings
is consistent with the value of low-fidelity prototypes for making design
changes with limited preparation \cite[Sec.~``Benefits of Low-Fidelity
Prototypes'']{perniceprototypes}.

V1 was a desktop-first, browser-based prototype implemented as a front end.
Its main screens were Home, Search, Library, content detail and the full
player. These screens supported connected task paths rather than only
showing isolated layouts. Users could search the sample catalogue, open an
item, change its saved state, find it in the Library and open unfinished
spoken audio at its displayed progress. This level of interaction allowed
the tests to examine labels, navigation choices, controls and feedback.

The catalogue and listening states represented the intended experience.
The prototype did not provide a complete streaming service, so opening the
player at an existing position was an interface response, not a test of
audio delivery. V2 retained this implementation scope and the main task
paths. It provided a revised interface for a second evaluation, while V1
remained available as the earlier version. Detailed screen descriptions and
visual comparisons belong in the Design chapter.

\section{Usability Testing}
\label{sec:method-usability-testing}

Two usability-testing rounds were conducted with three participants in each
round. P1, P2 and P3 tested V1, while P4, P5 and P6 tested V2. All six were
students, aged 20--26, who reported regular use of audio applications.
Their backgrounds included mainly music listening and combinations of music,
podcasts and audiobooks. These characteristics were relevant to the intended
student audience. The records describe participants' listening habits but do
not specify how they were recruited.

The evaluations used the interactive prototypes in a browser on a laptop,
within the project's desktop-use focus. The V1 plan identifies
Mittuniversitetet in Sundsvall as the location and specifies that the
prototype ran locally. The V2 plan also specifies a laptop and browser.
Participants' usual mobile or computer use was recorded as background
information; the sessions did not constitute a comparison between devices.

The group divided moderation and observation between its members. Yacoub
Dawli led the sessions and presented the tasks. Obid Alrhman Aljabi
documented participant behaviour and comments, while Sarshar Chavoshi Rad
recorded task times, clicks and navigation observations. This division
allowed the moderator to attend to the participant while the observers
recorded the interaction.

Each session began with an introduction to the university project and the
purpose of the test. After information about participation and consent, the
moderator asked which audio applications the participant normally used,
which audio types they listened to, how often they listened and whether
they normally used mobile devices, computers or both. The three tasks were
then presented individually. Participants were asked to explain what they
were looking for, what they expected to happen and anything they found
unclear while using the interface. This followed the think-aloud approach
introduced in Section~\ref{sec:research-evaluation}
\cite[Sec.~``Defining Thinking Aloud Testing'']{nielsenthinkaloud}.

The instructions described the intended goal without specifying the control
to select. For example, the unfinished-audiobook task did not tell
participants to use Continue Listening. This left their choice of route
available for observation. When prompting was needed, the moderator asked
neutral questions about what the participant was thinking or expected.
Participants were encouraged to try what made sense to them instead of
being immediately directed to a solution.

included checking that the saved item could be found again. The second
concerned returning to an existing listening position, while the third

The same core goals and completion criteria were used in the second round
so that the group could compare the two versions without replacing the
activities being examined. The wording allowed different valid routes to
the goal. Finding content through a category, for example, could still meet
the first task's criterion. The records could therefore distinguish task
completion from the particular route chosen.

The task records covered completion, elapsed time in seconds, click counts
and navigation paths, alongside notes on hesitation, incorrect actions and
any need for help. Relevant think-aloud comments accompanied these observations.
Completion related to effectiveness, while time, clicks and routes provided
information about effort and efficiency. Hesitation and comments helped
examine whether controls were discoverable and whether their feedback was
understood. These records supported interpretation of the measured values;
a time or click count alone did not explain a difficulty.

Post-test questions covered the overall impression, easy or confusing parts,
unclear labels, missing functions and suggested changes. The V2 records also
included questions about Save and discoverability. The group reviewed these
responses alongside the task observations in each round. This connected
participants' impressions to what happened during interaction. Individual
accounts, measurements and the comparison of results are presented in the
Results chapter.

\section{Iterative Evaluation}
\label{sec:method-iterative-evaluation}

After the first evaluation, the group brought together task records,
navigation paths and post-test comments to identify issues for redesign.
The V1 analysis linked each identified issue to supporting observations,
its effect on the tested task and a proposed action. The prioritised problem
list distinguished concerns about reaching or discovering task-related
content from smaller concerns about visual clarity. It also identified
parts of the interface to retain.

The resulting redesign focused on Continue Listening visibility, the height
of the Home introductory area and the clarity of the Save/Saved control.
These areas connected the recorded concerns to specific changes that could
be examined in another test. The basic navigation, categories, Library and
core task goals were kept consistent. This limited the scope of the redesign
and retained the main interaction context for comparison.

V2 was then evaluated with new participants using the procedure and tasks
described in Section~\ref{sec:method-usability-testing}. Using different
participants avoided repeating the tasks with people who had already learned
the V1 paths. The second-round analysis considered the same completion
criteria, measures, routes and participant feedback, including observations
related to the targeted changes.

This comparison concerned the tested prototype interactions. With three
different participants per round, differences between the records cannot
be attributed solely to the redesign or generalised to all students. The
purpose was to examine the revised design against the earlier concerns,
without assuming that changing the interface established an improvement.

\section{Research Ethics}
\label{sec:method-research-ethics}

The participant introductions explained that the group was evaluating the
interface, not the person using it. They described the university project,
the observation and written note-taking, and the intended use of the
information. Participants were told that taking part was voluntary, that
they could stop at any time and that they could raise questions or
discomfort. The moderator then asked whether they were comfortable
participating, and the records include agreement before the tasks.
This approach addressed informed and voluntary participation as discussed
in Section~\ref{sec:research-ethics} and in Vetenskapsr\aa{}det's guidance
\cite[pp.~62--63]{vetenskapsradet2024}.

The introductions stated that written notes would be anonymous and used for
the university project. The retained records identify participants as
P1--P6 instead of using personal names. Background information about
listening habits and device use provided context for the tests without
requiring names in the report.

Accurate reporting also informed the treatment of the material. The testing
records distinguish observations and participant comments from the group's
interpretations. This distinction is maintained when describing the method
and reporting the results, consistent with the responsibility to communicate
research honestly \cite[pp.~11--12]{vetenskapsradet2024}.

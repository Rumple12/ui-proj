\section{Initial Student Interviews and Sketch Feedback}
\label{sec:results-initial-interviews}

Before developing the high-fidelity prototypes, the group conducted five short interviews with students at Mittuniversitetet in Sundsvall. These were separate from the six participants in the later V1/V2 usability tests. Students described their audio habits, existing applications, difficulties and preferred features. The group also showed early interface sketches and asked for impressions and suggestions.

The first student used Spotify and YouTube, valued easy playlists and fast search, and described difficulty remembering where podcasts had been saved. The second student used Spotify, Storytel and Apple Podcasts, and wanted unified search and an obvious way to continue unfinished content. The third mainly used Spotify, and wanted music to remain easy to find without podcasts and audiobooks crowding the Home screen. The fourth mainly listened to podcasts and audiobooks, and wanted progress tracking, playback-speed control and other spoken-audio features. The fifth used Spotify and Audible and wanted one search entry point, clear filters and combined saved content.

The students' comments illustrated different needs. One said, ``I don't mind having different types of audio together as long as they don't become mixed up.'' Another said, ``The annoying part isn't opening another app. It's remembering which app I used.'' The music-focused student said, ``If everything is in one place, I still want control over what I actually see.'' These comments concern early expectations, not observations from the later prototype tests.

The group organised the notes into themes: fast search, a shared saved collection, clear separation of audio categories, resuming unfinished listening, control over unwanted Home content and additional features for spoken audio. Sketch-feedback questions also invited suggestions about missing controls and expected navigation. These findings helped prioritise the first prototype's shared Search, Library, categories and Continue Listening. Customising the Home screen, bookmarks, sleep timers and offline access were considered as later possibilities.

\section{Identified User Needs}
\label{sec:results-user-needs}

The project concept already prioritised finding content, saving it in a
shared Library and returning to unfinished listening. Keeping music,
podcasts and audiobooks recognisable was another starting requirement.
The testing records provided observations about how participants used
these functions and what they found unclear.

Finding and retrieving content involved more than one route. Participants
used Search or Music to locate Weezer, then saved the item and checked
Library. Their actions showed how the original finding-and-saving goal was
used in practice. Library also served another purpose for P3, who looked
there for the unfinished audiobook. Saved collections and unfinished
listening therefore overlapped in this participant's route through V1.

The feedback also identified specific priorities within the concept.
P2 and P3 did not use Continue Listening in the first test and later said
they had not noticed it. P1 suggested making Save more obvious. In V2,
participants recognised the changed Saved state and checked the item in
Library. These observations concerned finding the relevant control and
understanding what happened after selecting it. Category names were also
used directly during the tasks, and participants' post-test comments
described the separation between audio types as understandable.

\section{Usability Test 1 Results}
\label{sec:results-v1}

resuming an unfinished audiobook and browsing podcasts. This gave nine
completed tasks out of nine attempts, with no direct assistance from the
test leader. Table~\ref{tab:results-v1} presents the recorded completion
times and outcomes.

For the first task, P1 selected Search in the sidebar, while P2 opened
Search through the top search field. P3 opened Music and found Weezer among
the visible cards. Each participant opened its details, selected Save and
found the item in Library. Their routes differed, but all reached the
requested saved collection.

P1 noticed Play before Save and briefly paused before locating the saving
control. After saving, P1 understood the confirmation and opened Library
to check the result. P3 initially assumed the heart would add the item
somewhere, then named Library after reading the confirmation message.
In the post-test response, P1 suggested: ``Maybe make Save slightly more
obvious.'' No participant needed help to complete the saving task.

The audiobook task produced the largest spread in V1 times, from 19 to
35 seconds. P1 used Home, Continue Listening, the audiobook details and
Resume. P2 instead opened Audiobooks from Home, selected
\emph{The Left Hand of Darkness} and chose Resume. P3 went from Home to
Library, opened the same audiobook and selected Resume. The player opened
at the existing progress in all three cases.

Only P1 used Continue Listening directly. P2 scanned the audiobook cards
through the category route, while P3 passed the Continue Listening section
and searched in Library. P3 said during the task: ``Maybe unfinished things
are saved here.'' The progress information and Resume control then identified
the audiobook as unfinished. The recorded resume-task click counts were
two for P1, three for P2 and four for P3.

All three participants reached Podcasts. P1 and P2 selected it directly in
the sidebar. P3 accidentally selected Music first, noticed its active
highlight and immediately switched to Podcasts. This was the recorded
wrong action in that task; P3 still completed it without help.

Post-test comments matched the different audiobook routes. P2 said:
``I didn't immediately notice Continue Listening. I went to Audiobooks
instead.'' P3 also said the section had not been noticed and commented:
``Maybe make Continue Listening more obvious. The big banner above it takes
a lot of space.'' The main V1 observations were completed tasks through
several routes, an overlooked Home shortcut for P2 and P3, a brief hesitation
around Save for P1 and one category-selection error followed by immediate
recovery.

P4 used the top search field to find Weezer, and P5 selected Search in the
sidebar. P6 opened Music and found the item directly. All three selected
Save on the detail page, recognised the Saved state and opened Library to
check that Weezer appeared there. The music-category route therefore
remained available alongside Search in the second round.

P4 noticed Save immediately and described it as obvious. P5 briefly looked
between Play and Save before selecting it; the notes record a pause of
approximately one second. After the button changed to Saved, P5 said:
``Okay, now I know it worked.'' P6 selected Save without recorded hesitation.


All three participants used Continue Listening to resume the unfinished
audiobook. Their route was Home, Continue Listening, the audiobook details
and Resume. Each then reached the player at the existing position. P4
looked below the banner and selected the audiobook, P5 saw the section
without scrolling, and P6 referred to the displayed progress when identifying
unfinished content. The notes record two clicks and no hesitation for each
participant on this task.

For podcast browsing, P4, P5 and P6 each selected Podcasts in the sidebar.
The recorded times were four, four and three seconds respectively, with
one click each. No wrong category selections or difficulties were recorded
for this task in V2.

The post-test responses referred to both the targeted controls and the
general navigation. P4 described the sidebar and searching as the easiest
parts and said: ``No, Continue Listening was pretty visible.'' P4 also said
the heart and the word Save made the action clear. P5 named Continue
Listening and navigation as the easiest parts, while P6 named categories
and Continue Listening. All three described Save as understandable, and
none reported a confusing label.

The feedback also included suggestions beyond the tested tasks. P5 wanted
Recently Played as a possible later feature, and P6 suggested sorting the
Library. Neither suggestion described an obstacle that prevented task
completion. The V2 records therefore contain successful task outcomes,
direct audiobook-resumption routes and a brief saving pause, alongside
ideas for additional functions.

\section{Comparison Between Iterations}
\label{sec:results-comparison}

Task completion was the same in both rounds: nine out of nine attempts,
with no direct help required. The recorded differences concerned time,
route choice and hesitation rather than additional completed tasks. V1
participants took between 24 and 31 seconds to find and save Weezer,
compared with 21--24 seconds in V2. Audiobook resumption took 19--35 seconds
in V1 and 12--15 seconds in V2. Podcast browsing took 4--7 seconds in V1
and 3--4 seconds in V2.

Figure~\ref{fig:results-task-times} compares the average completion times.
Each average uses the three participant times for that task in its round.
The means are 27.7 seconds for finding and saving in V1 and 22.3 seconds in
V2; 27.0 and 13.3 seconds for resuming the audiobook; and 5.3 and
3.7 seconds for browsing podcasts. Values are rounded to one decimal place.

\begin{samepage}
The largest difference in average time was in audiobook resumption. Route
choice also differed on that task: one of the three V1 participants used
Continue Listening directly, compared with all three V2 participants.
The other V1 participants still completed the task through Audiobooks or
Library. Finding Weezer through Music occurred in both rounds, alongside
the two entry points to Search.
\end{samepage}

The saving records include a hesitation for P1 in V1 and a shorter pause
for P5 in V2. Both completed the task and checked Library. Category
navigation also reached the requested destination in both rounds; P3's
initial Music selection in V1 was followed by immediate recovery, while
no such wrong selection was recorded in V2.

The recorded completion times were lower in the second round, especially
for resuming the audiobook, and Continue Listening was used more often.
These are comparisons between two groups of three different participants;
the lower times alone do not show that the redesign caused the difference.
Across both rounds, all participants completed the tested tasks without
direct assistance.

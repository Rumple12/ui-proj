\chapter{Discussion}
\label{chap:discussion}
\section{Interpretation of Results}
\label{sec:discussion-results}

Both versions supported the main tasks for the participants who tested
them. Tables~\ref{tab:results-v1} and~\ref{tab:results-v2} show nine out of
nine completed tasks in each round, without direct assistance. This suggests
that the tested navigation and core interactions were understandable.
However, reaching a goal did not mean that every route was equally easy
to find.

The clearest difference concerned the unfinished audiobook. Only one of
the three V1 participants used Continue Listening directly; the others went
through Audiobooks or Library. All three V2 participants used Continue
Listening, and the average time fell from 27.0 to 13.3 seconds
(Figure~\ref{fig:results-task-times}). The second group found unfinished
content more directly and completed the task faster. The updated Home
screen may therefore have made the intended shortcut easier to notice.

Saving showed a smaller change. P1 briefly hesitated before finding Save
in V1. All V2 participants understood the Save/Saved state, although P5
briefly paused before saving. The clearer button appearance and retained
confirmation may have helped communicate both the action and its result.
The states shown in Figure~\ref{fig:design-save-comparison} provide a
visible reminder after the temporary confirmation disappears.

V1 already offered useful ways to complete the tasks. Search, Library and
the categories supported several successful routes, including finding
Weezer through Music. P3 also recovered immediately after selecting the
wrong category. Keeping these routes while changing a few specific areas
was a reasonable response to the findings. The remaining brief pause in V2
is a reason to keep observing the saving control, rather than assume that
every small difficulty has disappeared.

\section{Connection Between Theory and Findings}
\label{sec:discussion-theory}

The Home findings connect visibility with attention, as discussed in
Sections~\ref{sec:interface-principles} and~\ref{sec:cognition-mental-models}.
Continue Listening was available in V1, but its presence alone did not make
it an obvious starting point for P2 and P3. The large banner occupied space
above it. Reducing the banner and using a more direct heading gave unfinished
content greater prominence, as Figure~\ref{fig:design-home-comparison}
shows. This offers a plausible explanation for the different routes in V2,
without treating appearance as a complete explanation of behaviour.

Feedback mattered after an action as well. The changed Saved label and the
message naming Library told participants what had happened and where the
item could be found. P3's move to Library after reading the confirmation
is a concrete example. The active navigation highlight also helped P3
recognise an accidental category selection. These responses supported the
next decision instead of leaving the participant to guess the current state.

Consistency and recognition help explain why the alternative routes still
worked. Category names, titles and progress information gave participants
something to recognise. P3 expected unfinished content to be in Library
and could identify the audiobook there. Shared controls across the audio
types supported that route. Keeping the navigation and Library structure
in V2 preserved these interaction patterns alongside the clearer Home
shortcut.

The iteration also put the user-centred approach in
Section~\ref{sec:ucd-iteration} into practice. We chose the V2 changes from
observed hesitation, route choices and comments. This gave each change a
specific problem to address and a reason for another test. Personal
preferences about the appearance could not have told us whether listeners
would notice Continue Listening during a task.

\section{Strengths and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion-strengths-limitations}

A strength of the project was the connection between its stages. Early
sketches led to interactive prototypes, and the first test led to focused
changes that were tested again. The same core tasks gave both rounds a
common basis for comparison. Retaining V1 also made the design changes
visible. Together, the observations, comments, times and routes provide a
clearer account of the interaction than a finished interface alone would.

Only three participants tested each version, so the findings describe a
small set of experiences. Different people took part in the two rounds.
This avoided repeating the tasks with participants who had already learned
the V1 routes, but differences between people could also affect times and
choices. We therefore cannot attribute the lower V2 times only to the
redesign. The Home changes were introduced together, so the tests also do
not separate the effect of the smaller banner from the clearer heading.

The tasks covered finding and saving content, resuming an audiobook and
browsing podcasts. They did not examine every function or repeated use over
time. Testing took place in a laptop/browser setting, although many
participants normally listened on phones. The results therefore give little
information about the full mobile experience. The sample catalogue and
displayed progress also limited what could be tested: real audio delivery,
large collections, subscriptions and account handling were outside the
front-end prototype.

The five campus interviews were exploratory rather than representative of
all students, and the small sample limits how broadly the findings can be
applied. Participants described their listening habits, frustrations and
expectations, so the findings reflect self-reported experiences rather than
direct observations of prototype use. The group used handwritten notes
rather than complete transcripts, which limits the detail available when
reviewing the answers. Feedback on the paper sketches helped guide early
design priorities, while the later usability tests examined how participants
actually interacted with V1 and V2.

\section{Evaluation of Methods}
\label{sec:discussion-methods}

Starting with sketches was useful for considering navigation, content and
playback before building detailed screens. It left room to change the
arrangement without first completing an interactive version. The later
prototypes served a different purpose: participants could actually search,
save, browse and resume content. We could observe the connection between
screens and the result of an action, rather than rely only on opinions
about static layouts.

Think-aloud comments helped explain choices that the completion totals
would have hidden. P2 looked in Audiobooks and P3 expected unfinished
content in Library. Their comments and routes showed why the intended
Home shortcut needed attention. Task times and click counts added
information about the effort involved, while the observations showed where
participants paused or changed direction. Combining these records was
more useful than treating completion or time as the whole result.

The division between moderation and observation also helped the group
follow the interaction while recording it. Explaining voluntary
participation and using participant labels also addressed consent and
privacy, as described in Section~\ref{sec:method-research-ethics}.
In later work, we would retain these practices and make the link between
interview notes, requirements and design decisions easier to follow. A
larger and more varied participant group, with consistent task wording and
test conditions, would also give us a broader basis for reviewing the
interface.

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:future-work}

Radio was considered as another possible audio category, but the prototype focused on the selected music, podcast and audiobook tasks. Including radio would have expanded the content and navigation scope beyond the two evaluation rounds. A future version could explore radio browsing and playback with dedicated navigation and suitable usability tasks.

The next evaluation should include mobile interaction and a wider range
of listening habits. It should also ask people to return to saved or
unfinished audio after a break or over several visits. This would examine
whether Library and Continue Listening remain useful when users have to
recognise content later, rather than immediately after a task. More
realistic playback and retained progress across visits would support that
evaluation.

P5's suggestion of Recently Played and P6's suggestion of Library sorting
are reasonable ideas to explore. They were not needed to complete the
tested tasks, so they should be considered alongside further observations
of how people return to content. A small prototype extension could examine
whether these options help with that activity and whether their purpose
is clear alongside saved and unfinished items.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Omnisound explored how music, podcasts and audiobooks could share search,
saving and playback while keeping their content types recognisable. The
project identified a focused set of interaction priorities, explored
alternative layouts through early ideas and sketches, and developed two
interactive prototypes. This met the design and evaluation goals and helped
us understand how people used the main functions.

All participants completed the three core tasks in both usability-testing
rounds. The first evaluation nevertheless showed that Continue Listening
could be easier to notice and Save could be more prominent. We made focused
changes to Home and saving in V2, then tested the same goals again. The
second group used Continue Listening more directly and recorded shorter
task times. These findings support the value of the iteration for the
tested interactions, while remaining specific to the small groups and
prototype scope.

The main lesson was that making a function available does not guarantee
that people will find it when they need it. Watching participants use
Omnisound revealed difficulties that were easy to overlook while designing
the screens. Their actions and comments gave us concrete reasons to make
changes and a clear focus for testing the next version.
